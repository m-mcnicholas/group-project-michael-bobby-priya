Visible improvement unproven

Exact TEST commit 9945193 · Read-only review

My assessment: the Blender work improved recovery consistency and produced a useful technical reference, but the inspected browser changes do not establish a substantial improvement in visible fur realism.

Your observation that the fur behaves much like it did before is consistent with the source changes.

The central reason is that DuckPlan still renders and deforms essentially the same kind of strands. The new response system makes their timing more consistent with the approved button, but it does not introduce the richer physical bending, contact interactions, or collective pile behavior that made the Blender experiment potentially valuable.

Verified improvement: matching the existing button solver

In landing/fur-response.js, build() integrates the button's existing spring model at 240 Hz into a response atlas sampled at 120 Hz.

It carries source displacement, velocity, targets, compression, depression, and recovery timing.

The tests compare atlas results against DuckFurSurface.simulate() for 0, 280, 2,000 and 5,000 ms delays.

That is a meaningful software-engineering improvement. It addresses the earlier defect where the sheet used an unrelated analytic recovery equation.

But it is fundamentally about making the workspace match the existing button.

It does not necessarily make either one look more realistic.

Verified improvement: small strand appearance changes

The button shader now includes more compression/depression-dependent strand width and shading. The sheet shader also applies stronger tip narrowing, such as:

glsl
p += side * corner.x *
     mix(rootWidth, rootWidth * 0.36, corner.y);

The previous sheet tip was considerably broader.

These can make individual fibers appear narrower at the tips and change how they visually compress.

However, the overall representation remains similar.

What did not happen

The browser did not adopt the Blender reference's five-segment fiber mechanics, contact constraints, or strand geometry. It still uses its own original real-time rendering architecture.

That is the principal gap between the Blender-assisted upgrade you requested and what was delivered.

2. Severity-ranked reasons the fur still looks similar
P0 — The browser still bends each button strand as one straight shape

Source: landing/fur-material.js, VERTEX_SHADER, approximately lines 130–185.

The button shader calculates one root position, one displacement direction and a projected tip position.

Its basic structure is:

glsl
vec2 direction = naturalLean * heightScale
               + bend * compressionResponse;

vec2 pixel = rootPx
           + direction * a_corner.y
           + normal * a_corner.x * width;

This generates a tapered, elongated quad.

When the fur responds to pressure, that quad changes orientation, length and width. It does not bend through multiple independently moving joints.

Current button representation

A strand's entire shape follows one root-to-tip vector.

Proposed multi-segment behavior

Roots remain anchored while middle and tip segments respond differently.

These are conceptual geometry diagrams, not actual captures of either renderer.

Blender's contact probe uses five rooted segments per fiber. The browser button does not.

This means the browser cannot directly reproduce the probe's segment-level curvature and contact deformation.

This is the highest-value geometry improvement to investigate.

P0 — No dense-pile neighbor interaction was translated into the browser

The current button response operates primarily on individual fiber state arrays and local press profiles. The shared wave response aggregates them into 32 angular directions.

The inspected browser implementation does not introduce a coupled neighbor-contact system in which moving fibers mechanically displace, resist or compress nearby fibers.

Therefore, it cannot reproduce effects such as localized pile bunching, mechanical interlocking, frictional drag and contact-driven collective movement through a physical neighbor solver.

These mechanisms are plausible opportunities for more natural-looking fur. Their benefit at DuckPlan's short strand scale remains a visual hypothesis until tested.

The main Blender spring study deliberately resembles the approved JavaScript system. Agreement between them cannot independently validate physical realism.

The 64-fiber probe is more independent, but it is sparse relative to a dense short-pile fur surface. It does not establish what thousands of interacting fibers would look like, nor does it prove a biologically accurate duck-down model.

The Blender report records a minimum mean tip height of 4.133 mm from a 5 mm rest height, maximum lateral splay of 3.533 mm, and recovery to within 0.05 mm by 1.2 seconds.

These are measurements of the custom Blender experiment. They were not established as independently measured animal-fur properties.

P1 — Contact and brushing are still simplified

The existing pressProfile() and imprintPress() generate inward compression, outward splay and localized depression. That gives responsive feedback.

But the rendering does not fully express how a soft pile changes shape after a contact moves across it.

In particular, no browser-side mechanism was added for:

Curved strand deformation along the shaft

Accumulated directional combing and lay direction

Fiber-to-fiber friction and local bunching

Neighbor-induced compression

Contact-driven occlusion changes between adjacent strands

Those are potential causes of the perceived artificiality. I cannot rank their visible contributions without the actual recordings.

P1 — The current visual shading remains largely stylized

The shaders still construct fur appearance through root/middle/tip colors, directional lighting, alpha-softened silhouettes and compression-dependent shading.

Those are appropriate for performant WebGL2 rendering.

However, they are not a physically derived model of fiber scattering, self-shadowing or dense pile light transport.

The Blender work did not introduce such a model into the website.

The existing baked pile texture also remains a significant component of the expanding sheet's appearance, so geometric changes to its live strands may not dominate the visible result.

4. Would importing Blender geometry fix it?

Not by itself.

A .glb export could provide curved fibers, more detailed geometry and carefully authored reference shapes. But imported geometry without a meaningful real-time deformation model would still behave according to whatever simplified animation system controlled it.

It could improve the resting silhouette while leaving interaction nearly unchanged.

A better approach is to extract a small procedural strand model from the Blender reference:

Anchored root

One or two intermediate bend points

Freely moving tip

Compression-sensitive curvature

Directional friction and recovery

That can be represented by a lightweight segmented ribbon or curve in WebGL2.

The browser would retain its existing root distribution and material appearance, but use a richer strand-shape response.

The distinction is between importing what the strand looks like and transferring how the strand moves. Your complaint concerns the second.

5. Recommended next implementation — focus on visible behavior

I would avoid another broad Blender project. Instead, implement one narrowly scoped visual experiment, prove that it looks better, and only then expand it.

Priority 1 — Segmented bending

Add a multi-segment deformation option for representative fur strands. The base stays anchored while the middle and tip bend at different rates, allowing a genuinely curved response to pressure and brushing.

Priority 2 — Directional pile memory

Test a lightweight local combing field. Brushing should leave nearby fibers temporarily leaning in the direction of travel, with gradual viscoelastic recovery rather than immediate independent springback.

Priority 3 — Collective compression

Introduce a bounded neighborhood influence so compressed groups slightly affect surrounding strands. Test whether this produces a more coherent dent and outward redistribution without the cost of full pairwise fiber collision.

These should initially be experimental modes in Fur Lab, not changes forced across every DuckPlan button.

If they demonstrate a noticeable improvement, the resulting deformation model can be applied to both the button and the expanding sheet.

Preserve the original approved behavior unless there is a deliberate tradeoff

The existing button's appearance and interaction behavior were explicitly preserved in the Blender implementation.

But adding realistic curvature, combing memory or neighbor effects necessarily changes some of that behavior.

Codex should therefore maintain the approved mode as a fallback and offer the enhanced mode for visual comparison. Only promote an enhancement after confirming that it improves realism without damaging lettering, responsiveness, or UI readability.
